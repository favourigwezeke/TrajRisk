\section*{Introduction}

Drug-induced liver injury is the most common cause of acute liver failure in the Western world \cite{ref001,ref002}, and acetaminophen (APAP) overdose is the leading cause in the United States and the United Kingdom \cite{ref003,ref004}. High CYP2E1-dependent bioactivation in zone-3 hepatocytes produces the characteristic pattern of pericentral necrosis \cite{ref004,ref005}. As the liver regenerates, surviving hepatocytes can return toward baseline states or remain in persistent compensation for days \cite{ref006,ref007}. How these responses diverge at single-cell resolution remains unclear. Understanding this heterogeneity matters because hepatocyte regeneration helps determine outcomes after APAP-induced acute liver failure \cite{ref006,ref008}.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiles thousands of hepatocytes across injury and recovery \cite{ref009,ref010,ref011}, and trajectory inference orders them along a pseudotime axis relative to a reference state \cite{ref012,ref013}. Expression similarity is hard to interpret when injury-associated cells reacquire metabolic programs during recovery or persistent compensation \cite{ref010}. RNA velocity \cite{ref014,ref015} and lineage tracing \cite{ref016,ref017} offer directional information but require spliced and unspliced counts or lineage markers. Controlled injury studies provide an alternative constraint: sampling times orient transition graphs, while treatment labels distinguish exposure histories even when expression profiles converge. Injury and recovery comprise distinct temporal responses \cite{ref006,ref018,ref019}, motivating treatment-aware modeling of single-cell drug and toxicant time courses \cite{ref020,ref021,ref022}.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) propagate information over single-cell graphs to represent cell states \cite{ref023,ref024} and can complement per-cell transcriptional scoring of disease-associated programs \cite{ref025,ref026,ref027}. A graph shared across an entire dataset may obscure changes in transition structure between response phases. Multi-scale and multi-resolution architectures provide ways to represent scale-dependent structure outside biology \cite{ref028,ref029,ref030,ref031} and in medical time series \cite{ref032}. APAP responses progress through early stress, peak injury, heterogeneous recovery or compensation, and later resolution \cite{ref006,ref033,ref034}, within the broader sequence of inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling in tissue repair \cite{ref035,ref036,ref037}. Phase-specific graphs may therefore reveal structure that a single propagation scale does not resolve. Network-science analyses of directed edges \cite{ref038,ref039} and cell-state transitions \cite{ref040,ref041} offer a complementary way to examine where model-derived information concentrates within these graphs.

Here we present TrajRisk, which learns directed, treatment-aware transition graphs and integrates expression features across them with a multiscale graph neural network. The TrajRisk score estimates normalized Monocle 3 pseudotime in the mouse injury--recovery reference. It is a dimensionless measure of trajectory state, not a clinical prediction. A Random Forest \cite{ref042} learns transition weights at each scale from cell pairs labeled by nearest-neighbor matching. Parallel convolution branches aggregate incoming messages along the directed edges \cite{ref043}, using the learned weights directly in place of symmetric graph normalization \cite{ref044}. We evaluate the framework across four mouse APAP studies and compare human donors with APAP-associated acute liver failure (APAP-ALF) and healthy donors in shared mouse--human coordinates \cite{ref019}. We also examine orthologous gene signatures in human nuclei and spatial tissue \cite{ref045,ref046}. Finally, flux analysis identifies a candidate 24--48~h recovery gateway for experimental study.
