\section*{Discussion}

TrajRisk uses treatment-aware transition graphs to organize hepatocyte states during APAP-induced liver injury. Four graphs represent phases associated with metabolic and oxidative stress, inflammation, and recovery \cite{ref010,ref018,ref034,ref049}. Compared with expression-only XGBoost, TrajRisk had higher average precision at 24~h, whereas XGBoost had higher global AUROC and performed better at later time points. Examining both aggregate and within-time-point performance exposed differences that a single discrimination metric would miss \cite{ref050,ref051}. The graph provides a way to examine which cell states and transition programs contribute to the score.

RNA velocity \cite{ref014,ref015} and clonal lineage tracing \cite{ref016,ref052,ref053,ref054} offer complementary routes to directionality. Heterogeneous proliferation can affect lineage-based inference \cite{ref055}. TrajRisk uses the sampling axis and treatment annotations to orient edges, complementing methods based on velocity or learned embeddings \cite{ref041,ref056,ref057}. Two of the integrated datasets lacked the paired count layers needed for velocity estimation \cite{ref014,ref058}. Time and exposure constrain the graphs even when late APAP-treated cells resemble untreated hepatocytes \cite{ref010}. The RF weights describe transitions between matched expression states; the data do not track cell ancestry. Combining an expression-derived seed with graph messages follows related approaches to representing single-cell and regulatory networks \cite{ref059,ref060}.

Scale 3 (24--48~h) had the highest concentration of model-derived flux, while Scale 2 carried the largest total flux. The top-flux Scale 3 edges connect a small group of 24~h sources, many with elevated pseudotime, to predominantly lower-pseudotime 48~h targets. Earlier APAP studies described persistent compensation \cite{ref010} and p21-positive perinecrotic hepatocytes associated with impaired recovery \cite{ref061}. The concentration of flux in Scale 3 resembles restricted transitions in other dynamic systems \cite{ref062,ref063}. These edges define a candidate resolution gateway. Lineage tracing could test whether the source cells contribute to recovery, and targeted perturbations could test the associated programs. Removing the Scale 1 graph produced the largest changes in frozen-model scores, highlighting its contribution to prediction.

External mouse results varied across cohorts. Both TrajRisk configurations ranked APAP-treated cells above vehicle controls more accurately than the expression-only comparators at 2~h. The self-only configuration had the highest AUROC and average precision in that cohort. Expression-only comparators performed better on the two later cohorts and had stronger correlations with \textit{Cdkn1a}. These comparisons link the score to early injury and later expression states without implying a consistent advantage from graph propagation.

In human liver, the mouse-trained score distinguished APAP-ALF from healthy donors after joint unsupervised integration. This use of a reference relates to transfer learning \cite{ref064}, hierarchical annotation \cite{ref065}, and alignment through shared or learned homology \cite{ref066,ref067}. Cross-species single-nucleus studies likewise examine where disease-associated expression programs agree or diverge \cite{ref068,ref069}. Mouse references also provide a way to compare programs in human disease tissue \cite{ref067}. We used donor-level summaries for inference to account for the nesting of nuclei within donors \cite{ref070,ref071,ref072}.

The mouse-derived risk signature was higher in healthy human donors. Subtracting the recovery signature improved separation in the prespecified direction, but the net-signature comparison was not significant. Risk-signature genes include markers of mature hepatocyte identity and metabolism such as \textit{Mat1a} and \textit{Hnf4a}. HNF4\ensuremath{\alpha}-associated function can decline during acute injury \cite{ref073}, and the MAT1A/S-adenosylmethionine axis is altered in liver disease \cite{ref074}. Dedifferentiation \cite{ref073,ref075} and inflammatory reprogramming \cite{ref073,ref076} could contribute to the reversed signature direction between the mouse reference and human explants.

The risk signature was enriched at fibrogenic injury boundaries in Ben-Moshe tissue, where the original study described a regenerative niche and extracellular-matrix induction \cite{ref011}. It showed no pericentral enrichment in either Matchett dataset. Constitutive metabolic zonation \cite{ref077,ref078,ref079} differs from an injury boundary defined by fibrogenic remodeling. Cell loss, remodeling, and sampling time may also affect these expression patterns \cite{ref011,ref080,ref081}. Earlier sampling and donor-resolved analyses could test when the signature localizes to injury boundaries.

Human differential-expression overlap with mouse bottleneck-associated genes varied by scale. The overall 40.4\% counts distinct human genes. Mouse and human livers share some transcriptional responses and diverge in others \cite{ref082,ref083,ref084}. The mouse experiments resolve specific exposure and recovery windows \cite{ref085,ref086}, whereas the human samples were collected at transplantation \cite{ref019}. The relative contributions of sampling and species to the differences across scales remain open.

The strategy can be tested in other settings with multiple sampling times, a treatment contrast for seeding, and enough cells to estimate transitions. Perturbation screens \cite{ref087}, dense stress-response time courses \cite{ref088}, staged organoid atlases \cite{ref089}, and harmonized perturbation-response datasets \cite{ref090} provide such opportunities. Graph models already serve related tasks in single-cell state modeling, imputation, regulatory inference, and perturbation prediction \cite{ref024,ref091,ref092,ref093,ref094,ref095,ref096,ref097}. The current model uses four scales chosen from APAP injury biology \cite{ref006,ref010,ref034} and a seed trained to separate 0~h from 6~h hepatocytes. Applying the method elsewhere will require defining the temporal windows and reference states for that system.

\subsubsection*{Limitations of the study}

The reference evaluation was transductive. Training and validation cells shared graphs whose pair features include their pseudotime, although only training-cell targets entered the loss. The primary checkpoint was selected using validation AUROC, while the GNN ablations used a separate development split. Some ablations also changed more than one model component. AUROC confidence intervals summarize cell resampling within each cohort; the external and within-time-point comparisons were not tested pairwise.

External cells contributed to unsupervised integration. The two later mouse Reference-KNN projections also used treatment labels to select anchors. Testing generalization to a new cohort requires an independently fitted reference. External reference edges use Gaussian similarity in scVI space, as in latent-space reference mapping \cite{ref064,ref098,ref099}, because query cells lack the reference pseudotime required by the Random Forest. Human nuclei also differ from mouse cells in RNA capture and transcript representation \cite{ref100}. The controlled mouse experiment defines time after a fixed APAP dose \cite{ref101,ref102}, whereas clinical explants represent heterogeneous stages of illness \cite{ref019}. Excluding four human donors with fewer than 300 nuclei left the donor separation qualitatively unchanged.

The spatial tests pool spots, so their p values summarize the profiled sections. The Scale 1 KEGG neurodegeneration terms likely reflect shared mitochondrial and oxidative-stress genes rather than disease-specific expression \cite{ref103,ref104}. Human gene overlap uses nominal p values and does not require agreement in fold-change direction. A background matched for hepatic expression would help establish how much overlap exceeds chance \cite{ref105,ref106}. Extending the binary treatment design to multiple perturbations or doses would require a different graph and seeding design \cite{ref107,ref108}.

TrajRisk organizes heterogeneous hepatocyte states across sampled phases of injury and recovery. Its transition graphs can help prioritize the cell states and gene programs to test in studies of liver repair.
